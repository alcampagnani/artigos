Introdução Envolvente

When a foreigner dies without a will in Brazil, the family rarely expects what comes next. They assume the estate plan signed back home will simply carry over. It usually does not. Brazil applies its own rules to any property physically located inside the country, and those rules can reorder who inherits, how much, and when.

Picture an American who bought an apartment in Rio years ago. He passes away in Florida. His U.S. will leaves everything to his second wife. Yet the Rio apartment does not transfer automatically. A Brazilian court must open a separate probate, and Brazilian law decides whether the wife, the children from his first marriage, or both walk away with a share.

This gap catches people off guard every week. Heirs discover that an asset they counted on is frozen, that a stepchild they barely knew now holds a legal claim, or that the surviving spouse receives far less than the home country promised. The shock is rarely about the money alone. It is the realization that a foreign system now controls the family's future, on its own terms and its own timeline.

This guide explains what actually happens in that situation, in plain terms. You will learn the statutory order of heirs, why the marriage property regime quietly changes the math, and how the Brazilian probate works. None of this guarantees a specific result. It does give you a clear map before grief and bureaucracy collide. Think of it as the briefing your family wishes someone had handed them earlier.

Why Brazilian Law Controls Property Located in Brazil

Start with the rule that surprises most families. Real estate and other assets situated in Brazil fall under Brazilian jurisdiction, full stop. A foreign will, a foreign trust, or a foreign court order does not move a Brazilian apartment by itself. The transfer happens through a Brazilian proceeding.

The Brazilian Constitution and the Code of Civil Procedure reserve succession of assets located in Brazil for Brazilian authorities. So even when someone dies abroad with a perfectly valid foreign estate plan, the Brazilian-situated property still requires local probate. Two parallel processes can run at once: one in the home country for the assets there, and one in Brazil for the assets here. They do not talk to each other automatically, and neither one finishes the other's job.

This matters precisely because so many families assume the opposite. They imagine one global estate, settled in one place, by one lawyer. In reality, a bank account in São Paulo and a beach house in Bahia answer to Brazilian courts even if every other asset sits in California. That local layer is the part nobody planned for, and it is the part that decides the inheritance of the property here.

Which law decides who inherits? That answer comes from the Introductory Law to Brazilian Norms (LINDB), article 10. It points to the law of the country where the deceased was domiciled at the time of death. So if an American was domiciled in the United States, U.S. law may govern the substance of the succession in principle.

But article 10 carries a powerful exception. When the deceased leaves a Brazilian spouse or Brazilian children, the law more favorable to them prevails. Brazilian courts compare the foreign rule against the Brazilian rule and apply whichever gives the local family member more.

Scenario Law that may govern succession Who actually decides
Foreigner domiciled abroad, no Brazilian heirs Law of the deceased's domicile (LINDB art. 10) Brazilian court still handles the Brazilian assets
Foreigner with a Brazilian spouse or child The law more favorable to the Brazilian heir Brazilian court applies the comparison
Asset is real estate in Brazil Brazilian jurisdiction over the asset is exclusive Brazilian court only

Who Inherits When a Foreigner Dies Without a Will in Brazil

Now suppose Brazilian law applies, either directly or because it favors a Brazilian heir. When someone dies without a will in Brazil, the Civil Code fills the gap through what it calls sucessão legítima, or intestate succession. The law itself writes the will the person never wrote.

Article 1.829 of the Civil Code sets a strict ranking. Heirs in a higher class exclude those below them. The order runs like this:

  • First, descendants (children, then grandchildren), inheriting alongside the surviving spouse in many cases
  • Second, ascendants (parents, then grandparents), again alongside the surviving spouse
  • Third, the surviving spouse alone, when there are no descendants or ascendants
  • Fourth, collateral relatives up to the fourth degree (siblings, nephews, uncles, cousins)

So children almost never get cut out. Brazilian law protects them through a concept called the legítima, the forced portion. At least half of the estate is reserved by law for these necessary heirs, meaning descendants, ascendants, and the surviving spouse. A person cannot freely give away that half, even with a will. Without a will, the protection simply applies in full, and the whole estate distributes along these statutory lines.

This is one of the sharpest differences from common-law systems. An American can often disinherit an adult child entirely. Under Brazilian law, that adult child holds a guaranteed claim to part of the estate. In an intestate estate, the law treats those children as protected heirs no matter how distant the relationship had become. Articles 1.836 through 1.839 then govern how ascendants and collateral relatives step in when there are no descendants, ranking parents above grandparents and siblings above more distant relatives.

The surviving spouse holds a special place. Article 1.830 adds a condition: the spouse inherits only if the couple was not separated at the time of death, or had been separated for no more than two years through no fault that points against the survivor. A truly estranged spouse can lose the inheritance right.

⚠️ A COMMON MISTAKE: Foreigners often assume the surviving spouse automatically takes everything, as in many U.S. states. In Brazil, the spouse usually shares with the children. Counting on full inheritance can leave a widow or widower with far less than expected.

When descendants and a spouse inherit together, article 1.832 fixes the spouse's share. The spouse receives a portion equal to each child's share, and never less than one quarter of the estate when the spouse is also the parent of those children. The arithmetic shifts depending on how many children there are and whether they descend from the surviving spouse.

How the Marriage Property Regime Quietly Changes the Math

Here is the detail that trips up nearly every foreign family. Before you even ask who inherits, you must ask what the couple already owned together. The marriage property regime decides that, and it sits underneath the entire succession.

Brazil separates two things people often blur. Meação is the surviving spouse's own half of the marital property, earned by being married under a sharing regime. It is not inheritance at all. The other half, the deceased's half, is what passes to the heirs.

Article 1.829 ties the spouse's inheritance right to the regime. Under comunhão parcial (partial community, the default for most marriages), the spouse already keeps their meação of shared assets, then may also inherit from the deceased's separate property. Under comunhão universal (universal community) or mandatory separação de bens, the rules change, and sometimes the spouse inherits nothing on top of their half precisely because they already hold so much.

Property regime Spouse's existing half (meação) Does the spouse also inherit?
Partial community (default) Half of assets acquired during the marriage Yes, from the deceased's separate/private assets
Universal community Half of essentially all assets Generally not, alongside descendants
Mandatory separation of assets None from this regime Generally inherits alongside descendants

For a foreign couple, the regime may have been set by the law of the country where they married, or by a prenuptial agreement, or by Brazilian default rules if they married here. Determining the correct regime is often the first real fight in a cross-border estate, because it changes the size of the pie before anyone divides it.

Consider how dramatically the result can swing. Take a couple married under partial community who bought a Florianópolis apartment together during the marriage. The surviving spouse already owns half of that apartment as meação. Only the other half enters the estate and gets split among heirs. Now imagine the same couple under mandatory separation of assets. The surviving spouse owns no automatic half, so the entire apartment enters the estate, but the spouse then inherits alongside the children. Same apartment, same family, completely different outcome. When a foreigner dies without a will in Brazil, this single variable often decides whether the widow keeps the home or shares it.

💡 PRACTICAL TIP: Locate the marriage certificate and any prenuptial agreement early. In a Brazilian estate, these two documents often matter more than the foreign will, because they fix the meação before inheritance even begins.

The Brazilian Probate (Inventário) Step by Step

A Brazilian estate does not settle itself. The assets located here must pass through inventário, the formal probate that identifies heirs, values the estate, pays taxes, and transfers title. Until it finishes, the apartment stays in the deceased's name and cannot be sold or refinanced.

There are two paths. The judicial route runs through a court and is mandatory when heirs disagree, when a minor or legally incapable heir is involved, or in several other situations. The extrajudicial route happens at a notary's office (cartório) and works only when every heir is an adult, capable, in agreement, and represented by a lawyer.

A representative summary of how the process unfolds:

Stage What happens Typical concern for a foreign family
Open the probate File the petition; name an estate administrator (inventariante) Deadline pressure; documents abroad
Identify heirs and assets Prove the family tree and map every Brazilian asset Foreign documents need translation and legalization
Pay the ITCMD tax State inheritance/transfer tax, rates vary by state Cash needed before transfer; planning matters
Settle and divide Court or notary confirms each share Disputes between spouse and children surface here
Transfer title Register the new owners at the real estate registry Final step that frees the asset

Foreign heirs face extra friction. Documents issued abroad, such as death certificates, birth certificates, and marriage records, typically need an apostille under the Hague Convention and a sworn translation into Portuguese. Heirs who live overseas usually grant a power of attorney to a Brazilian lawyer so they do not have to fly in for each step.

There is also the question of proving the family tree itself. A Brazilian notary or judge needs documentary evidence of who the heirs are, in a format Brazilian law accepts. A U.S. birth certificate without an apostille will not do the job. Gaps in paperwork stall the whole estate, sometimes for months. In these intestate cases, much of the delay traces back not to legal disputes but to missing or improperly legalized documents. Getting that paperwork in order early is one of the most practical things a family can do.

📈 WHY TIMING MATTERS: Several Brazilian states historically expected probate to open within roughly 60 days of death, with potential tax surcharges for delay, and deadlines vary by state. Waiting also means the property sits frozen, sometimes for years, while heirs cannot sell or use it.

The inventariante, the person who administers the estate, carries real duties. They manage the assets, represent the estate, and answer to the court or notary. Choosing the right one, often with legal guidance, prevents a lot of later conflict.

Protect Your Family Before a Foreigner Dies Without a Will in Brazil

You have seen how much can go sideways. The good news is that almost all of it responds to planning done in advance. When a foreigner dies without a will in Brazil, the family inherits the law's default plan. With preparation, you can shape a far smoother outcome.

A few concrete steps make a real difference:

  • Confirm your marriage property regime and keep the prenuptial agreement, if any, with your important papers
  • Consider a Brazilian will for your Brazilian-situated assets, coordinated with your home-country plan so the two do not contradict each other
  • Keep an organized file of property deeds, the matrícula numbers from the real estate registry, and tax records
  • Talk to your heirs so they know an asset exists in Brazil and know whom to call

A will signed in Brazil for Brazilian assets cannot override the forced portion reserved for necessary heirs. It can, however, organize the disposable half, name an executor, clarify intent, and dramatically reduce the friction your family faces. Coordination between your two estate plans is the part most people skip and later regret.

Why does coordination matter so much? Because two well-meaning documents can contradict each other. A U.S. will that leaves "everything" to one beneficiary collides with Brazilian forced heirship the moment a Brazilian asset is involved. A Brazilian will drafted in isolation might accidentally revoke or conflict with the home-country plan. The goal is one coherent picture, where each document handles the assets it can lawfully reach and stays silent on the rest. A lawyer who works across both systems can spot these collisions before they harden into a courtroom dispute.

It also helps to think about liquidity. Brazilian inheritance tax, the ITCMD, generally must be paid before assets transfer, and heirs sometimes lack the cash to cover it. A property-rich, cash-poor estate can stall simply because no one can fund the tax bill. Planning for that, whether through savings, insurance arranged in the home country, or other means, keeps the estate moving instead of frozen.

✅ A SHORT CHECKLIST FOR FAMILIES WITH BRAZILIAN ASSETS: - Know which law would govern your succession under LINDB article 10 - Identify your necessary heirs and the forced portion they are owed - Confirm your marriage property regime and the spouse's meação - Keep apostilled, translated documents ready before they are urgent

No lawyer can promise a particular result, and you should be skeptical of anyone who does. What good planning offers is clarity, fewer surprises, and a family that is not negotiating with a foreign bureaucracy in the middle of mourning.
