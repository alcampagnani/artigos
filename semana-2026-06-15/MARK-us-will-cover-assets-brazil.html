Why a US Will in Brazil Is Not Enough on Its Own

Many Americans assume a properly signed estate plan travels with them. Unfortunately, a US will in Brazil does not flip a switch that transfers your local property. Brazil is a separate sovereign country with its own civil code, its own courts, and its own succession rules. When you own assets there, those assets answer to Brazilian law first.

The core issue is jurisdiction. Brazilian courts claim exclusive authority over certain matters, and real estate sitting in Brazil is at the top of that list. A US judge cannot order a São Paulo registry to change the name on a property title. Only a Brazilian proceeding can do that. So even a flawless will from Florida or California stops at the border when it touches Brazilian land.

That said, your foreign will is not worthless. It can serve as evidence of your intentions and, in some cases, be recognized through a local process. The mistake is treating it as a finished solution rather than a starting point.

Think of it like driving across a border. Your license proves you are a competent driver, yet the new country still sets its own speed limits and road rules. Your US will proves your intent, but Brazil still governs how property on its territory actually changes hands. The document opens the conversation; it does not close it.

There is also a timing problem most people overlook. Brazilian institutions, from banks to property registries (cartórios de registro de imóveis), will not act on a foreign document at face value. They want a Brazilian order or notarial act that they can file and rely on. A US will in Brazil, however well written, simply does not give them that authority. So heirs who arrive expecting a quick signature often discover a months-long process instead.

Brazilian Probate (Inventário): The Step You Cannot Skip

Probate in Brazil is called inventário. It is the formal process that identifies a deceased person's assets, settles debts and taxes, and transfers ownership to the rightful heirs. For property located in Brazil, this step is mandatory. There is no shortcut that lets your heirs simply wave a US will in Brazil and take title.

The inventário can happen in two ways. When all heirs are adults, agree with one another, and no valid will complicates things, the family may use an inventário extrajudicial, handled at a notary's office (cartório) and usually faster. When heirs disagree, a minor is involved, or a will must be interpreted, the case goes to court as an inventário judicial. Court cases naturally take longer.

Timing varies widely. A cooperative extrajudicial inventário with organized paperwork might wrap up in a few months. A contested judicial case, especially one involving heirs scattered across two countries, can stretch on much longer. Brazilian law also sets a deadline to open the proceeding after death, and some states apply a tax penalty when families miss it. In other words, waiting "until things calm down" can quietly cost money.

For Americans, distance multiplies every friction point. Heirs living in Texas or New York cannot pop into a São Paulo notary office whenever a signature is needed. They rely on a local attorney acting under power of attorney, on couriered documents, and on translations. Each of these steps adds days or weeks. Building that pipeline before it is urgent is one of the smartest things a property owner can do.

A few practical realities catch foreign families off guard:

  • Taxes come first. A state-level inheritance tax, the ITCMD (Imposto sobre Transmissão Causa Mortis e Doação), must be calculated and paid before transfers complete. Rates vary by state, generally up to 8%.
  • Documents need translation and legalization. US death certificates, wills, and IDs typically require sworn translation and an apostille under the Hague Convention.
  • Foreign wills may need recognition. A will made abroad can sometimes be admitted, but a Brazilian court or notary still controls how local assets pass.

Skipping or delaying inventário has real consequences. Until it finishes, heirs cannot sell the property, access frozen accounts, or fully use what they inherited. Years can pass while a single apartment sits in limbo. Meanwhile, condo fees, property taxes (IPTU), and upkeep keep accruing, often paid out of pocket by relatives who cannot yet touch the estate that owes them.

There is a relationship dimension too. Inventário forces heirs to agree, and grief does not always make people agreeable. When siblings in different states disagree about selling the family beach house, the process stalls. Clear instructions and a coordinated plan, prepared while you are alive, reduce the room for those disputes. That is planning working quietly in your favor.

Jurisdiction: Why Brazilian Courts Control Brazilian Property

Here is the legal backbone behind everything above. Brazil's Code of Civil Procedure (Código de Processo Civil) addresses when Brazilian courts have authority. [SUGGESTED EXTERNAL LINK ON "Brazil's Code of Civil Procedure (CPC), Article 23": "https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13105.htm"]

Under CPC Article 23, Brazilian courts hold exclusive jurisdiction over two situations that matter deeply to American owners. First, lawsuits concerning real estate (immovable property) located in Brazil. Second, the confirmation of a will and the succession of assets situated in Brazil, even when the deceased was foreign and lived abroad.

Read that again, because it is the heart of the matter. A US will in Brazil cannot bypass these rules. No foreign court ruling, however valid in the United States, will be honored by Brazil to transfer Brazilian land or distribute Brazilian-situated assets. That authority belongs to Brazil alone.

⚠️ COMMON MISTAKE TO AVOID: Some families spend money completing a full US probate, then assume a Brazilian registry will simply accept the American court order. It will not. The Brazilian asset still requires a separate proceeding inside Brazil. Paying twice for the same goal is avoidable with planning.

What does this mean in practice? Consider the difference between asset types.

The takeaway is clean. Brazilian assets follow Brazilian procedure. Your American documents handle your American property. When your estate spans both countries, you almost always need coordinated planning on both sides.

It helps to understand the reasoning behind the rule. Real estate is permanently tied to a nation's territory, so most countries, including the United States, treat the location of land as decisive for succession. Brazil simply enforces this principle firmly through its exclusive jurisdiction. A foreign judgment can sometimes be recognized in Brazil for other purposes, through a process at the Superior Tribunal de Justiça, but that path does not extend to transferring title to Brazilian real estate or distributing Brazilian-situated assets. Those stay home.

For an American owner, the practical lesson is to stop thinking in terms of one master document. Picture two parallel tracks running side by side: one for your US estate, one for your Brazilian estate. They should communicate and avoid contradiction, but each track needs its own engine. A single will pretending to run both tracks usually stalls on the Brazilian side.

Forced Heirship: How Brazil Limits What You Can Give Away

Even when a will is recognized, Brazil restricts how freely you can distribute your estate. This concept is forced heirship, known locally as the legítima. It is one of the biggest shocks for Americans, who are used to broad testamentary freedom in most states.

Under the Brazilian Civil Code, certain relatives are "necessary heirs" (herdeiros necessários): descendants (children, grandchildren), ascendants (parents, grandparents), and the surviving spouse. The law reserves half of your estate, the legítima, for these heirs. You may only freely dispose of the other half, called the parte disponível, through your will.

So imagine you write a US will in Brazil leaving everything to a friend, a charity, or a single child. For your Brazilian assets, that wish collides with the legítima. The protected heirs can claim their reserved half regardless of what your document says. A foreign will cannot quietly override these mandatory shares.

💡 PLANNING TIP: Forced heirship does not mean you lose all control. You still direct the freely disposable portion. Smart structuring of that half, combined with clear documentation, lets you honor your intentions within the rules instead of fighting them.

Key points Americans should internalize:

  • The reserved half is mandatory for descendants, ascendants, or spouse when they exist.
  • You control the other half and can leave it as you choose.
  • Disinheritance is narrow. Brazilian law allows it only in specific, serious circumstances defined by statute, not by personal preference.
  • Mismatched plans cause litigation. A foreign will ignoring the legítima invites disputes that drain the estate.

A quick example shows how this plays out. Suppose an American widower owns an apartment in Florianópolis and has two adult children. He wants to leave the Brazilian apartment entirely to his new partner. Under Brazilian rules, his children, as descendants, are entitled to the reserved half of that Brazilian asset. He can leave the partner the freely disposable half, but he cannot cut the children out of the legítima for property in Brazil. A US will saying otherwise will not change that outcome.

There is also a currency and valuation angle. The legítima is calculated on the value of the estate, so how the Brazilian apartment is appraised matters to every heir's share. Disagreements over valuation are a frequent source of conflict. Documenting your assets clearly, and getting local guidance on how Brazilian law measures the reserved portion, removes a lot of that uncertainty before it becomes a fight.

This is exactly why a document drafted only with US rules in mind can backfire across the border. American freedom of testation and Brazilian forced heirship pull in different directions. A Brazilian attorney helps you reconcile both so your plan survives contact with reality.

Building a Cross-Border Plan That Actually Holds Up

You now understand the obstacles. The good news: with proper planning, a US will in Brazil can coexist with a sound Brazilian strategy, and your heirs can avoid the worst delays. The goal is alignment, not duplication that contradicts itself.

Most experienced practitioners suggest one of two approaches. Some clients keep their US will for American assets and add a separate Brazilian will covering only their Brazilian property, carefully drafted to respect the legítima. Others keep a single will but have it reviewed by a Brazilian attorney to confirm it can be recognized and probated locally without conflict. The right choice depends on your assets, your family, and your goals.

✅ CROSS-BORDER ESTATE CHECKLIST: - Inventory every Brazilian asset: property, accounts, inherited shares. - Confirm whether your current will conflicts with forced heirship rules. - Decide between one coordinated will or a separate Brazilian will. - Prepare documents for translation, apostille, and recognition in advance. - Identify a trusted local representative with power of attorney. - Plan for ITCMD inheritance tax in the relevant state.

Watch for traps that quietly sabotage good intentions. Two wills that revoke each other are a classic disaster; if a Brazilian will says "I revoke all prior wills," it can wipe out your US plan, and vice versa. Drafting language must be precise so each document covers only its intended assets. This coordination is technical work, not a do-it-yourself project.

Language and culture add hidden complications that planning can soften. Brazilian legal documents are in Portuguese, deadlines follow Brazilian calendars, and notary practices differ from US conventions. An American executor unfamiliar with cartórios can feel lost quickly. A local attorney who communicates in English bridges that gap, translating not just words but procedures, so the family understands each step rather than reacting to it.

Think about the difference in lived experience. Family A did nothing in advance, so after a death the heirs spent over a year gathering apostilled documents, fighting over valuation, and paying fees on a frozen apartment. Family B reviewed everything two years earlier, aligned a Brazilian will with the legítima, and pre-arranged a power of attorney. When the time came, their inventário moved steadily and predictably. Same country, same rules, very different outcomes, all because of preparation.

Outcomes are never guaranteed in any legal matter, and anyone promising certainty should raise concern. What good planning offers is reduced risk, fewer surprises, and a smoother path for the people you love. That is a realistic, valuable goal.
