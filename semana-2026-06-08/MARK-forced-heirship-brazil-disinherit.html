So can you disinherit a child or a spouse in Brazil? Sometimes, but only on narrow legal grounds spelled out in the Civil Code. What Forced Heirship in Brazil Actually Means

Forced heirship in Brazil is the legal principle that certain close relatives have a guaranteed right to inherit part of your estate, no matter what your will says. These protected relatives are called herdeiros necessários, or "necessary heirs." The concept lives in the Brazilian Civil Code (Lei No. 10.406/2002, the Código Civil), and it shapes nearly every cross-border estate that touches Brazilian property.

The core rule sits in Article 1.789 of the Civil Code. When a person dies leaving necessary heirs, that person can only freely dispose of half of the estate by will. The other half is locked away for the necessary heirs.

The Two Halves of Every Estate

Think of your estate as split into two equal portions:

  • The legítima (reserved portion): 50% of the estate that must go to your necessary heirs. You cannot give this away by will to outsiders.
  • The disposable portion (porção disponível): the remaining 50%, which you can leave to anyone — a friend, a charity, one specific child, or your spouse.

This 50/50 split is the heart of forced heirship in Brazil. Americans often assume a properly signed will overrides everything. It does not. A Brazilian judge will honor your will only for the disposable half. The reserved half follows the rules of the Civil Code regardless of what you wrote.

Why This Catches Americans Off Guard

In most U.S. states, you can disinherit an adult child with a single sentence. Brazil treats that same sentence as void for the reserved portion. The law assumes family obligations survive death, and it protects descendants, ascendants, and spouses from being left with nothing.

This difference is not a technicality. It changes how your whole plan should be built. An American who drafts a will the way they would in Texas or Florida may unknowingly create a document that a Brazilian court partly ignores. Worse, conflicting instructions between a U.S. will and Brazilian law often spark exactly the family fight the person hoped to avoid. The reserved portion is rooted in public policy, so a Brazilian judge cannot waive it even if everyone seems to agree at first.

💡 TIP: If you own property in Brazil, never assume your U.S. will settles the matter. Brazilian succession over Brazilian real estate is generally decided by Brazilian courts under Brazilian law. A coordinated estate plan in both countries usually saves your heirs years of conflict.

Who Counts as a Necessary Heir in Brazil

Before you can plan around forced heirship in Brazil, you need to know exactly who the law protects. Article 1.845 of the Civil Code names three categories of necessary heirs: descendants, ascendants, and the surviving spouse. Anyone outside these categories is not a protected heir and has no claim to the reserved portion.

The Three Protected Categories

Category Who It Includes Typical Priority
Descendants Children, then grandchildren, great-grandchildren First in line; closest generation inherits
Ascendants Parents, then grandparents Inherit only if there are no descendants
Surviving spouse Husband or wife (status depends on the marital property regime) Inherits alongside descendants or ascendants

Descendants come first. If you have living children, your parents do not inherit as necessary heirs. Only when there are no descendants do ascendants step into the protected class. The surviving spouse holds a more complex position, because their share depends heavily on the marriage's property regime.

Children Are Treated Equally

Brazilian law makes no distinction between children born inside or outside marriage, nor between biological and legally adopted children. The Federal Constitution and the Civil Code guarantee equal inheritance rights to all children. An American father who fathered a child in Brazil cannot favor his "marital" children over that child in the reserved portion.

Where the Spouse Fits

Whether your spouse inherits — and how much — turns on your regime de bens (marital property regime). Under comunhão parcial (partial community of property), the default for most marriages, the spouse already owns half of jointly acquired assets and may also inherit a share of private assets. Under full separation of property, the spouse's inheritance rights change again. This is one of the most misunderstood corners of forced heirship in Brazil, and it deserves a careful look.

Keep in mind that the spouse's "ownership" half from the property regime is separate from inheritance. First the marriage's property split happens, then succession applies to the deceased's remaining share. A surviving spouse can therefore walk away with their own marital half plus an inherited slice of the rest. For an American married in Brazil, this layered system frequently produces a larger spousal outcome than they expected, especially when descendants are also in the picture and competing for the same reserved portion.

⚠️ ATTENTION: Many Americans believe a prenuptial agreement signed in the U.S. automatically controls inheritance in Brazil. It often does not. The Brazilian property regime, not your prenup, frequently determines your spouse's reserved share. Verify this before assuming anything.

The Reserved Portion: How the 50% Works in Practice

Understanding the legítima is where theory meets real numbers. The reserved portion is not a vague guideline; it is a precise mathematical share that Brazilian probate courts calculate before honoring any will. Forced heirship in Brazil turns on getting this calculation right.

Calculating the Legítima

Article 1.846 confirms that the legítima belongs to the necessary heirs and equals half of the estate's net value. Courts first total your assets, subtract debts and certain expenses, and then divide what remains into two halves. One half is the reserved portion, split among your necessary heirs. The other half is yours to assign by will.

Suppose an American leaves a Brazilian estate worth R$2,000,000 net, with two children and no spouse complications. The math looks like this:

Estate Component Amount Who Decides
Total net estate R$2,000,000 —
Reserved portion (legítima) R$1,000,000 Civil Code; split equally between the two children
Disposable portion R$1,000,000 The deceased, by will
Each child's guaranteed minimum R$500,000 Protected by law

Even if the will tried to leave everything to a new spouse, each child would still receive R$500,000 from the reserved half. The will would only govern the disposable R$1,000,000.

Lifetime Gifts Count Too

You cannot dodge forced heirship in Brazil by giving assets away while alive. Article 1.847 and the rules on colação require that significant gifts made to descendants during your lifetime be brought back into the calculation. The court adds those gifts to the estate's value to check whether the reserved portion was respected. Strategic gifting is possible, but it must stay within the disposable portion to survive a challenge.

A Narrow Window for Flexibility

The disposable half is your real planning space. Within it, you can favor one child, provide extra for a spouse, support a partner who is not a legal heir, or leave a legacy to a cause that matters to you. Smart Brazilian estate planning concentrates on using that half wisely rather than fighting the reserved half, which rarely works.

Disinheritance in Brazil: When Cutting Someone Out Is Legal

Now to the question that brings most people here. Yes, Brazilian law does allow deserdação (disinheritance), but only on specific, serious grounds and only through a formal process. You cannot disinherit a necessary heir simply because the relationship soured. Forced heirship in Brazil yields only when the heir has committed conduct the law treats as grave.

The Legal Grounds for Disinheritance

Disinheritance is governed by Articles 1.961 through 1.965 of the Civil Code. Article 1.961 allows necessary heirs to be excluded from even the reserved portion when one of the legal causes is present. Those causes connect to the rules on indignidade (unworthiness) in Articles 1.814, plus the specific grounds listed in Articles 1.962 and 1.963.

The recognized grounds include serious situations such as:

  • An attempt against the life of the person leaving the estate
  • A serious physical assault against them
  • Grave slander or a serious criminal accusation against them
  • Acts of cruelty, abandonment, or a serious moral offense
  • In some cases, abandonment of a parent suffering from mental illness or serious disability

These are deliberately narrow. The law does not recognize estrangement, lifestyle disagreements, or "we just don't get along" as valid reasons. A son who never calls, a daughter who married someone you dislike, a child who moved abroad and rarely visits — none of that qualifies. The grounds focus on grave wrongs against the person leaving the estate or, in limited cases, serious abandonment of a vulnerable parent. Courts read these provisions strictly because disinheritance strips away a constitutionally backed family right.

The Process Is Strict

Requirement What the Law Demands
A valid will Disinheritance must be declared expressly in a will (Article 1.964)
Stated cause The will must name the specific legal ground for disinheritance
Proof in court After death, the cause must be proven in a judicial proceeding (Article 1.965)
Time limit The heir benefiting from the disinheritance generally must prove the cause within four years

If any step fails — no will, no stated cause, or unproven facts — the disinheritance collapses and the heir keeps the reserved share. Brazilian judges scrutinize these cases closely, because the consequence is severe.

🔍 INSIGHT: Disinheritance and "unworthiness" (indignidade) overlap but differ. Disinheritance must be declared by the deceased in a will and proven afterward. Unworthiness is raised by interested parties after death, usually for criminal acts against the deceased. Both are exceptions, not loopholes.

Forced Heirship in Brazil vs. U.S. Testamentary Freedom

For Americans, the gap between the two systems is the real source of confusion and risk. Understanding the contrast helps you plan instead of assuming your home-country instincts will carry over. Forced heirship in Brazil and U.S. testamentary freedom start from opposite philosophies.

Two Different Starting Points

The United States generally champions freedom of disposition. With limited exceptions — notably spousal "elective share" rules in many states — you can structure your estate as you wish. Most states let you disinherit an adult child outright. Brazil starts from family protection instead, reserving half the estate for necessary heirs as a matter of public policy.

Issue United States (general rule) Brazil
Disinheriting an adult child Usually allowed Only on narrow legal grounds
Reserved share for children None in most states 50% reserved portion (legítima)
Spousal protection Elective share in many states Tied to marital property regime
Control over your estate Broad testamentary freedom Limited to the disposable half
Lifetime gifts to avoid heirs Often effective Brought back via colação

Which Law Governs Your Brazilian Assets

Here is the practical core. Brazilian real estate and assets located in Brazil are generally subject to Brazilian succession law and Brazilian probate (inventário). A U.S. will can sometimes be recognized, but it must pass through a Brazilian court and cannot override the reserved portion for Brazil-based assets. So even a flawless American estate plan may be partly rewritten by forced heirship in Brazil once Brazilian property enters the picture.

Planning Across Two Systems

None of this means you are powerless. It means you plan with the rules, not against them. Practical, lawful strategies often include using the disposable portion deliberately, choosing a marital property regime intentionally, structuring ownership thoughtfully, and coordinating separate wills for U.S. and Brazilian assets so they do not contradict each other.

Timing matters too. The cleanest plans get built while you are healthy and clear-headed, not in a rush after a diagnosis or a family rift. Brazilian probate already moves slowly, and a contested estate can stretch on for years while assets sit frozen. A well-drafted Brazilian will, aligned with your U.S. documents and reviewed by a lawyer who handles cross-border succession, spares your heirs that ordeal. It also reduces the chance that a disappointed relative challenges the estate and drags everyone into litigation.

Conclusion: Plan With the Law, Not Against It

Forced heirship in Brazil reshapes the assumptions most Americans carry about wills and inheritance. Half of any Brazilian estate is reserved for necessary heirs — descendants, ascendants, or a spouse — and that reserved portion stands firm against a will. Disinheritance exists, but only on the narrow grounds in Articles 1.961 through 1.965, declared in a will and proven in court.

The good news is that you still control a meaningful half of your estate, and intelligent planning concentrates on using that freedom well. The mistake is assuming your U.S. will quietly governs your Brazilian property. It usually does not.
